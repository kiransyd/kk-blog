# The One Thing I Learned About Speaking Under Pressure

I’ve sat through hundreds of pitch meetings. Some went great. Others… I still cringe thinking about.

The difference wasn’t preparation. I prepared for all of them.

The difference was how I handled the unexpected question. The one that derailed my mental script. The one where my brain went blank.

Matt Abrahams calls this “spontaneous speaking.” I call it the thing that separates good founders from stressed ones.

## Key Takeaways

- Anxiety spikes when you lose your train of thought — the fix isn’t memorising more, it’s having a structure to land on
- A simple “What I’m hearing is…” buys you three seconds of processing time without looking lost
- The best answers in my experience come from admitting “I need to think about that” — customers respect honesty more than a fumbled guess

## Why Your Brain Freezes (And What Actually Helps)

When someone asks you something you didn’t expect, your brain treats it like a threat. Cortisol spikes. Blood leaves the prefrontal cortex. Suddenly you can’t remember your own product’s pricing.

Most advice tells you to “breathe” and “stay calm.” That’s like telling someone in a burning building to “relax.”

What actually works: a fallback structure.

Abrahams teaches a simple one: problem → solution → benefit. I’ve been using a variant for years in demos and investor calls. When I get a curveball, I say:

*“That’s a great question. The short answer is X. The longer context is Y. Does that cover what you needed?”*

It’s not clever. It’s reliable. And reliability under pressure is worth more than eloquence.

## The Real Insight (Not in the Video)

Abrahams focuses on technique. But the deeper issue for founders is **ego**.

We want to sound smart. We want an answer immediately. So we rush, fumble, and dig ourselves into a hole.

I’ve started telling my team: “If you don’t know, say you don’t know. Then ask a clarifying question.”

On a recent customer call, someone asked about a compliance feature we haven’t built yet. Old me would have danced around it. New me said: “We don’t support that today. But tell me what you’re trying to solve — might be something we can build or work around.”

They appreciated the honesty. We found a workaround. They’re still a customer.

## Practical Takeaway

You can’t script every conversation. But you can script your **recovery**.

Write down three fallback structures that feel natural to you. Something like:

1. **Acknowledge** (“Good question.”)
2. **Frame** (“The main thing to know is…”)
3. **Check** (“Does that help?”)

Practice them in low-stakes situations — team standups, casual chats. By the time a VC or a big customer throws a curveball, your response won’t be a scramble. It’ll be muscle memory.

That’s the only preparation that matters for the moments you can’t prepare for.
