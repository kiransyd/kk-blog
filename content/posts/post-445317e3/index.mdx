# Write what your customers ask in sales calls

Most content advice is written for companies with marketing teams and six-figure budgets. Keyword research. Pillar pages. Top-of-funnel thought leadership. We tried that. It didn't move anything.

What moved the needle was simpler and cheaper: writing down what customers were already asking us in sales calls.

**Key takeaways**
- Your last 10 sales calls tell you exactly what to write. The questions customers ask before buying are the content that converts.
- Don't guess keywords. Your customers hand you the exact phrases they search for.
- One deep post that answers a real buying question beats ten generic posts aimed at "awareness."
- Case studies about *how you fixed a problem* outperform explainers about *the problem itself*.

## Why most content fails bootstrapped startups

Content marketing works when you can afford to be patient. You spend six months building authority on a broad topic. You nurture readers who aren't ready to buy. Eventually some of them convert. That model requires budget, time, and a sales cycle that matches.

I don't have that [VERIFY: can confirm timeline — GoVisually is X years in?]. Most bootstrapped founders don't. We need leads that are ready to act. We can't write twenty posts hoping one lands.

The problem is most content strategy advice assumes you have resources to play the long game. Every SEO agency talks about "top of funnel" content — generic "what is label compliance?" pieces. But for a small team, that's a waste. You compete with companies that have entire content teams. You won't beat them on volume.

You can beat them on specificity.

## How we found what to write

We stopped writing what the marketing playbook said and started writing what came up in customer conversations. Every week I take the sales calls I've been on. I pull out the questions that came up more than once.

- "Can you check FDA compliance automatically?"
- "How do you handle multiple reviewers on packaging revisions?"
- "What happens if we miss a label change deadline?"
- "We're using email for approvals. Is that really a problem?"

These are actual questions from prospects [VERIFY: adjust if not exact]. They're not hypothetical. These are people who already know they have a problem and are looking for a solution. They're searching for those exact phrases.

So we wrote posts that answered those questions directly. Not "5 tips for better packaging compliance" — generic and forgettable. But "How to catch FDA label errors before printing: a workflow for regulated food brands." Specific. Actionable. Addressing the exact question.

That post outperformed everything we'd written before by a wide margin [VERIFY: can include specific metric if available].

## The format that works

We tested a few formats. Here's the pattern that keeps coming up:

1. State the problem exactly as the customer phrased it.
2. Explain why it's harder than it looks (real constraints, not theory).
3. Show a concrete example — ideally with a diagram.
4. Walk through how we handle it. Tools, steps, trade-offs.
