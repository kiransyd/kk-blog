I saw a video titled "Scaling this $60M Tech Startup in One Meeting."

It's a founder of Slang AI ($68M hospitality software, well funded) sitting down with someone to get go-to-market strategies. One meeting.

I've been doing this for over a decade. Built products that died. Built ones that paid the bills. Currently running GoVisually — bootstrapped, several years in, pivoting from creative review to compliance software.

I've never scaled a company in one meeting. I've killed products in one meeting though.

**Key takeaways:**
- No single meeting fixes go-to-market — it's iterative and messy
- Advice that works for $60M funded startups usually breaks for bootstrappers
- The real unlock is talking to customers, not consultants
- Your GTM strategy is just your product-market fit hypotheses made visible

## "One meeting" is a great headline, bad reality

I don't know the specifics of that session. Maybe the advice was genuinely useful. But framing it as "one meeting to scale" sells the wrong story to founders who are struggling.

Most GTM advice you read is written for companies that raised $10M and need to spend it. It's about playbooks, funnels, and "growth loops." It assumes you have a team to execute, a budget for ads, and time to iterate.

Bootstrapped founders don't have that. We have three people, a product that barely works, and a bank account that runs out in six months.

I wasted a year on GTM advice from funded founders when I was at Competeshark. Spent $5k on ads, got 20 signups and zero retention. The "scaling playbook" doesn't work when your churn kills you before you learn anything.

## What actually works for small teams

I've learned three things from running GoVisually and talking to hundreds of customers directly:

**1. Your first GTM is your product. Not your sales pitch.**  
When we pivoted toward compliance for CPG, I didn't hire a salesperson. I packaged the product around a specific problem — catching label errors before FDA audits — and handed it to three early customers. I sat in their office and watched them use it. Every bug, every confusing button, every question they asked became a product change.

That's your GTM: fix the thing that makes them say "this isn't ready yet."

**2. One customer willing to pay beats 100 leads who "might."**  
I'd rather have three companies paying $2,000/month and giving me feedback than 300 trial users who never convert. Because those three tell me exactly what's broken, and I can fix it before I spend on marketing.

Most GTM advice skips this. It talks about "lead scoring" and "nurture sequences." If you're bootstrapped, your nurture sequence is: email the customer directly, ask what's wrong, then build it.

**3. The "meeting" never ends.**  
We're years in. I still have weekly calls with customers. I still get feature requests that make me rethink our entire roadmap. The idea that a single strategy session sets your trajectory is romantic but false.

Scaling is a series of small corrections. Every week you adjust based on what customers say and what the numbers show. You pivot the product, the pricing, the positioning, and sometimes the market.

## What the video probably covered (and what's missing)

I haven't watched the full video, but I've seen enough of these to guess. They talk about:
- Top-of-funnel channels
- Sales playbooks
- Pricing tiers
- Hiring a VP of Sales

All useful — if you have a product that's proven and a team to execute.

What's usually missing:
- How to survive the first 24 months when your "strategy" fails and you have to change course
- How to price when you don't know your value yet (I use: charge low for the first X customers, raise aggressively)
- How to fire customers that don't fit your future
- How to say "no" to a feature that a big customer wants but that would destroy your focus

I learned the last one the hard way. At Pricify, we built a custom integration for one retailer. Took six months. Then they changed their mind. We'd neglected everything else. Company dead.
